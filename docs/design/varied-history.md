# Varied history

Lifecycle: **agreed** (18 November 2026, Jason with Design, #22). Not built: #52, #53 and #54 build it.

Pillar 1 (`direction.md`): history sets the shape, not the script. The great financial events of 1907 to 1938 come about when they did, but each game has its own timing and depth, so a player who knows the real calendar has an edge and never a sure profit. This page says what varies, by how much, and how.

## Why timing alone is not enough

Holding a short costs about 4% a year plus dividends. If the Crash always fell inside a known window, a short opened early and held would still win almost every game. What makes a bet uncertain is a rise that sometimes runs longer and higher (early shorts get burnt, as in 1928), and a fall that is sometimes smaller than the market already priced in. The City prices in the fall it expects; each game's real fall lands around that, sometimes milder.

## How it works

- **Across the whole economy.** The Depression is not a market event: the same curve sets fares, cargo, running costs, wages and ship prices. Every one of them, the share market and the newspapers follow the same per-game dates, so the shares and the trade never disagree (pillar 4).
- **Drawn once, kept in the save.** A save without the draw plays exactly today's history; no save is reset.
- **Hidden.** No screen, adviser, forecast or newspaper line gives the drawn numbers away. The player reads the signs: the rumours, the warnings, the mood.
- **A fixed-history switch for the tools**, so checks can run today's history and compare.

## Left fixed

- The Great War, the Stock Exchange closing in 1914 and the 1921 turn: the whole economy is built on those dates, and the war's coming was known.
- Laws on their real dates, such as the Companies Act of November 1929.
- Events that already vary: the 1912 disaster, the random panics from 1936.
- The yearly emigration tables for 1907 and 1908.
- **Consols** stay on the real yields. A drift of up to 0.4 points was considered and dropped: the real yield falls 1.67 points from 1929 to 1935, so even the worst case would gain about half over six years. The bet always wins; that is accepted as history, and its size is judged in the balance pass (#55).
