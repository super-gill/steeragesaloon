## 2. Fuel oil (bunker oil)

GAP. No opened source gave a British bunker oil price per ton for any year. What I have:
- 1919 US comparison (Hurley, US Shipping Board): "in normal times oil fuel for such a voyage might be either a little cheaper or a little dearer than coal"; on a round-the-world run a motor ship used 1,446 t oil (cost about $12,940) vs a coal steamer 8,500 t coal (about $41,275).
- Mauretania after 1922 oil conversion: 600 t oil/day vs 1,000 t coal/day before (so oil about 0.6 t per t coal for same work).
Suggestion: model oil price as roughly parity with coal per ton-equivalent-of-work, then derive from coal. Mark as design assumption.

## 5. Second-hand prices and the 1921 crash

- Standard ships 1919: sold at ~19% above cost (see above). 14 second-hand ships sold for £890,000 (~£64,000 each), 1919 (Hansard 19 May 1919).
- Cambridge (US, built 1919 for $1.04m) was among 200 ships sold to Ford for $1,706,000 in total (~$8,500 each) and scrapped 1925: illustrates the collapse to scrap value.
- Baltic (1904, 23,876 grt) sold for scrap 1933 together with Megantic for £33,000.
- Sailing ship William Mitchell sold for breaking 1927 for £2,100.
- Norway: newbuilt tankers late 1920s about NOK 650,000 equity vs wartime ship prices NOK 3-4 million; 1920 freights dropped by about three quarters; roughly half the world fleet idle at worst in 1921 (Springer chapter).
- GAP: per-dwt second-hand index 1914-1921. Glasgow thesis Table 3.8 covers 1916-1926 but not extractable.

## 7. Insurance

**1914 state war risks scheme (verified):** announced 4 Aug 1914 (Lloyd George). State reinsured **80%** of hull war risks written by the owners' mutual war-risk clubs; clubs kept 20%, bore all admin costs. No premium on voyages current at outbreak (cover until arrival plus 10 clear days). On new voyages the Government set the premium, **between 1% and 5%** per voyage, taking 80% of it. From 9 Sep 1914 hull values for reinsurance were capped at **first cost less 4% per year depreciation**. The Mutual War Risks Association ran until Aug 1917, when requisition left no insurable pool and the state in effect took 100%. The state made a profit of about **£16m** on WW1 reinsurance (Shinwell, 1939). 1939 scheme repeated 80% reinsurance, cost less 4% p.a., no peacetime premium.
**Peacetime hull premium:** only one illustrative figure found: "£5 per cent net" on a £10,000 line (Spectator 1925), with rates cut hard by post-war overcapacity while repair costs had doubled. GAP: a reliable per cent per annum series for 1900-1939. Suggest 3-6% p.a. as a game range, marked as design estimate anchored on that one quote.

## 8. Requisition (Blue Book / T.99) and losses

- Blue Book rate example: **about 9s per gross ton per month**, which Runciman equated to **5s 9d per deadweight ton per month** (Hansard 17 Feb 1916). Rates were fixed below market: neutral ships then earned about 40s/dwt/month.
- Share requisitioned: about one fifth of British fleet by Feb 1915; claims of "nearly two thirds" and 59% of one owner's fleet by Feb 1916; 43% of overseas-trade vessels by 1916 (USNI 1945).
- Lost requisitioned ships: war risk borne by the state (state carried 100% for requisitioned ships). Valuation cap: first cost less 4% p.a. (applies to state reinsurance from Sep 1914). Exact T.99 compensation formula: GAP.
- WW2 comparison (Hansard 18 Mar 1940): British requisition hire ~4s 6d per dwt per month (maybe rising to 6s-7s) vs neutrals 16s; losses paid at pre-war insured value in cash plus current value held in trust for replacement within 5-7 years after the war.

## 9. Excess Profits Duty and control

| Accounting period | EPD rate |
|---|---|
| First year (from Aug 1914 start) | 50% |
| To 31 Dec 1916 | 60% |
| 1 Jan 1917 - 31 Dec 1918 | 80% |
| 1919 | 40% |
| 1920 to end (repealed 1921) | 60% |
Standard: excess over pre-war profits (best two of last three pre-war years) or percentage on capital. Yield £140m (1916-17), £220m (1917-18), £285m (1918-19), £290m (1919-20), £219m (1920-21), including munitions levy. Shipowners paid it like others; no shipping-specific rate found.
Shipping Controller: post created 10 Dec 1916 (Sir Joseph Maclay), abolished 1921. Wages 1919: AB £5 10s/month pre-war to £11 10s plus £3 war bonus; junior officers/engineers £7 10s to £16 (Hansard 19 May 1919).

## 10. Canal dues and state support

- Suez dues per net ton (gold francs): 1911 laden 7.25, ballast 4.75; 1931 laden 6.65, ballast 3.325 (Hansard 14 Apr 1931).
- Lusitania/Mauretania: £2.6m government loan at 2.75% over 20 years; £75,000 a year each subsidy plus mail contract £68,000.
- North Atlantic Shipping Act 1934: £3m to finish No. 534, £1.5m working capital, £5m for second ship (total £9.5m).
- British Shipping (Assistance) Act 1935: £2m tramp subsidy for 1935; £10m scrap-and-build loans.

## Reliability note
Strong: Hansard coal export prices 1910-1929, 1914 scheme terms (80%, 1-5%), EPD rates, 1920-39 freight index (NBER/FRED), liner costs for Lusitania/Mauretania (Liverpool Museums). Medium: Wikipedia liner costs and fuel figures, Shipping Wonders (1930s popular work). Weak/illustrative: single-quote insurance rate, 1920 "37s 4 3/4d" (basis unclear), US ship prices used for wartime inflation. Main gaps: fuel oil prices, pre-1920 annual freight index, British new-build and second-hand price series, peacetime hull rate series, T.99 schedule.
