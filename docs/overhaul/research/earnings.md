## What lines earned

- **Mauretania, 1910**: receipts £659,000, ship expenses £325,000, gross operating profit £297,880 before depreciation and overheads ([Encyclopedia Titanica](https://www.encyclopedia-titanica.org/mauretania-revenue-profit-analysis-1910.html)). On a cost of £1.3m to £1.8m that is 16% to 23%, for the flagship of the richest trade in a good year. Her receipts were 37% to 50% of her cost a year.
- **White Star**: £848,000 profit in 1907 on a fleet book value of £4.85m (about 17.5%, less on original cost); under £300,000 in the 1908 slump (under 6%); £1.07m in 1911 ([Encyclopedia Titanica](https://www.encyclopedia-titanica.org/olympic-titanic-britannic-an-issue-of-finance.html)).
- **The Atlantic giants in 1934** lost money before depreciation: Olympic's receipts £23,104 a round trip against £27,962 disbursements; Aquitania, Berengaria and Majestic similar ([Chirnside](https://markchirnside.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2022/11/RMS_Olympic_Retirement.pdf)).
- **Royal Mail** made trading losses from 1926 ([Royal Mail Case](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Mail_Case)); White Star Line Ltd was wound up in 1935 with a deficit of £11m ([Allen Collection](https://benjidog.co.uk/allen/White%20Star%20Line.php)).
- **Tramps and cargo**: in the late 1920s operating costs ran 50% to 100% above pre-war while charter rates were about pre-war in money (Economist index 112 in 1923, 102 in 1927, 93 in 1928) and cargo companies' profits were "ground to pieces" ([NBER](https://www.nber.org/system/files/chapters/c4958/c4958.pdf)); 214 tramp companies earned only £8m of the £18m depreciation due in 1929-35 ([Hansard 1937](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1937/feb/01/british-shipping-continuance-of-subsidy)).
- **Cost shares** of a tramp voyage: capital 40%, port charges 25%, coal 20%, wages and provisions 10%, other 5% ([NBER w9531](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w9531/w9531.pdf)). Pre-1914 emigrant liners sailed 30% to 40% full ([Keeling](https://ebha.org/ebha2008/papers/Keeling_ebha_2008.pdf)).

## Bands used (inference from the above)

A new liner's operating return on her cost, before depreciation and interest: about 12% to 18% in good years (1905-07, 1910-13, 1927-29), 7% to 11% in average years, 0% to 5% or worse in a slump (1908-09, 1921-22, 1930-35). Express liners lower, as prestige ships. Tramps swing wider: 8% to 12% in normal pre-war years, far more in 1915-20, little in the 1930s.

## Where the game stood, and what changed

Measured with a new ship of each kind on her natural trade (`costshare` probe, July of the year):

| | 1910 before | 1910 after | 1928 before | 1928 after |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Emigrant ship, 11,000 tons | 16% | 13% | 41% | 18% |
| Passenger-cargo, 9,000 tons | 1% | 1% | 16% | 9% |
| Express, 40,000 tons | 4% | 4% | 8% | 7% |
| Tourist liner, 19,000 tons | | | 32% | 18% |
| Cargo liner, 7,500 tons | 23% | 17% | 25% | 15% |

Before the war the game was near the sources. After it, three things were wrong, and 0.39.1 changes each:

1. **Ships of the 1920s were designed as densely as those of 1905**: about 5 tons a berth for an emigrant ship, when third class had moved into cabins and new ships carried far fewer people a ton. A design's steerage and tourist berths now need more room as the years pass from 1914 to 1924.
2. **The post-war trade was shared by fewer ships than the pre-war fleets it was sized from.** Steerage and Tourist Third demand eases to seven tenths and six tenths between 1921 and 1924.
3. **Freight rates after 1921 settled where pre-war growth in trade had left them**, about 1.6 times 1900 against prices; they now settle at 1.15.

And new passenger and cargo ships cost more to build (about £38 to £53 a ton for emigrant and tourist ships in 1928, nearer the £51 to £61 of the sources).

Left as it was: **old ships bought second-hand before the war** still return about 18% to 31% a year on their asking price (the best of a quarter's list up to 50% or 60%), so an owner who buys whatever head office advises still grows very fast to 1914 (KI-065). Their prices are already at or above the period's (£5 to £8 a ton for an old steamer); the gap is in what an old ship earns against a new one, and the start of the game is balanced around the Morven, built in 1881.

**Correction (0.39.2):** the advisor's growth to 1914 does not come from old ships as such but from the boom-year steerage trade, new ships and old; see `advisor-growth.md`.
